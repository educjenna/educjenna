Candidate Statement

Candidate statement for the doctoral dossier.

Introduction

Education changes the world, and I have witnessed technology at the center of that change. As technology transforms education, my research asks how it can support learning without becoming the learning itself. I first confronted this question as an early-career teacher in Seoul when COVID-19 began, and I now pursue it in the era of AI. Across these shifts, I have learned in practice that learning should come first and technology second (Kolb, 2017). This principle has shaped my broader aim to design technology-supported learning experiences that scaffold learners’ thinking toward greater independence. I am particularly interested in young learners and novices, including pre-service teachers (PSTs), who are still developing the knowledge and strategies needed to become independent learners. My goal is to become a faculty member at a research university who supports these learners across a continuum of scaffolds, from embodied learning that makes abstract ideas tangible to self-regulated learning that helps learners manage their own thinking with AI, while opening the black box of how learners think and learn along the way.

Goals for Research

Scaffolding Learners’ Thinking

Scaffolding helps learners do what they cannot yet do alone, and its support fades as responsibility transfers to the learner (Pea, 2004; Wood et al., 1976). A key question guiding my research is How can technology scaffold learners’ thinking without taking over the thinking they need to do themselves? I investigate this question through two complementary approaches: 1) through the body (embodied cognition) and 2) through AI (self-regulated learning). Through the body, I examine how embodied learning can make abstract ideas tangible through gestures, objects, and physical interaction. Through AI, I examine how learners can develop the self-regulation needed to manage their own thinking while using generative AI (GenAI), including deciding when to use AI support and when to rely on their own reasoning. Together, these strands examine how technology can provide support while progressively positioning learners to take greater responsibility for their own learning.

1. Embodied Cognition: Making Abstract Ideas Tangible

Embodied learning can make abstract ideas tangible (Georgiou & Ioannou, 2019). As a teacher, and in my master’s study on virtual reality and spatial ability, I saw young children struggle with ideas they could not see or touch. How computers count and how machines learn are just as invisible to them. Because understanding is grounded in perception and action (Barsalou, 2008), gestures and objects can make such processes tangible when they are congruent with the concept (Skulmowski & Rey, 2018). Our study with first graders at Bloomfield Elementary showed that such congruence often needs to be scaffolded (under review). Yet embodied computational thinking research has largely targeted programming concepts (Kwon et al., 2024). I extend this work to the area of computing and AI. In SILICON, we design lessons in which middle school students and their families act out binary numbers and logic gates and use number cards and counting chips for binary addition. In the Robot Arm project, we designed lessons with Grade 5 science teachers in which students explore machine learning with Teachable Machine and then experience embodied AI with MediaPipe and a robot arm. Together, these projects aim to extend embodied design beyond programming to how computers and AI actually work, offering design principles for turning invisible computing processes into actions young learners can perform, and classroom- and community-ready lessons that teachers and families can use.

2. Self-Regulated Learning: Managing Thinking with AI

Whether AI scaffolds learning or invites overreliance depends less on the tool than on how learners use it. In AI Goes Rural, we designed chatbots for rural high school English classes. In an argumentation unit, tenth graders’ AI literacy improved, yet some relied on the AI (Kwon et al., 2025); in a career preparation unit, the same AI was a scaffold for some students and a substitute for others (Kwon et al., 2026). Because many students used the AI as an answer generator, we constrained it in a persuasive writing unit so that it gave feedback without rewriting student text at all. Constraining the tool, however, was not enough. Cognitive offloading remained the most frequent pattern, and few students kept agency over their revisions (under review), echoing what Fan et al. (2025) call metacognitive laziness, in which learners let AI feedback replace their own self-monitoring. These experiences shifted my focus from constraining what AI can do to scaffolding how learners decide when and how to use it. Co-designing an AI-supported project-based unit at Ben Davis University High School, where students used AI to analyze local transit data and redesign bus routes, further sharpened my view that the goal is not simply to restrict AI, but to help learners decide what cognitive work to retain and what to hand over (work in progress).

3. First-Authored Study: Designing Scaffolds for Self-Regulated AI Use

Supporting learners’ decisions about what to hand over to AI requires understanding how they regulate their own thinking. Self-regulated learning (SRL) explains this regulation and why novices struggle with it (Zimmerman, 2002). In Winne and Hadwin’s (1998) COPES model, learners judge feedback by comparing it with their own standards, the criteria they hold for good work. Novices such as PSTs, with limited pedagogical content knowledge, may lack those standards, so fluent AI evaluations go unchecked and invite them to offload effortful reasoning (Tankelevitch et al., 2024). Research on PSTs confirms this risk. They often treat GenAI-generated lesson plans as finished products with minimal edits (Tran et al., 2025) and overestimate their quality (Kalenda et al., 2025). Yet hybrid human–AI regulation models describe how regulation is shared between learners and adaptive systems (Järvelä et al., 2023; Molenaar, 2022) without specifying how an individual learner decides what to do with an AI evaluation, and existing work has mostly urged PSTs to evaluate AI outputs critically (Cai et al., 2025) rather than designing environments that support that evaluation. My first-author design-based study addresses this gap. Drawing on interviews with experienced instructors and undergraduate learning assistants about lesson planning, I designed an AI prototype that prompts PSTs to compare AI feedback with their own standards and to accept, adapt, or reject it with a pedagogical rationale. I implemented it in two course sections in Spring 2026 and a revised version in three sections in Fall 2026.

Goals for Teaching

My teaching goal is to help future teachers choose technology for the sake of their students’ learning, not for its novelty or convenience. My classroom in Seoul, where good materials and high usage did not necessarily guarantee learning, taught me to ask why before which tool (Kolb, 2017). Since Fall 2025, I have taught EDUC-W200: Teaching with Technology as an associate instructor, and I have also served as a teaching assistant for the graduate course EDUC-R521: Instructional Design and Development. W200 covers knowledge from TPACK (Mishra & Koehler, 2006) to intelligent-TPACK (Celik, 2023). Students begin with frameworks for meaningful technology integration, such as SAMR (Puentedura, 2006) and Triple E (Kolb, 2017), then examine how technology can support evidence-based assessment, and finally try different AI tools to create teaching materials and review what they produce. To help them move from frameworks to practice, I draw on cognitive apprenticeship (Collins et al., 1989). My K–12 classroom experience allows me to model technology decisions from my own classroom in Seoul, such as software and coding camps, game-based learning, and STEAM project-based learning, explaining why I chose each technology. Students then learn by doing with coding robots, immersive tools, and a makerspace, while I coach them with specific feedback on their lesson plans. When they create teaching materials with AI, they review the outputs and explain what they kept, revised, or rejected, articulating their own pedagogical reasoning. Support then fades as they make their own design decisions in their ePortfolios. Students have told me they value the hands-on experiences and specific feedback most. As I have not yet received formal course evaluations, I plan to gather them to refine my teaching.

Goals for Service

My goal in service is to build and sustain the communities in which teaching and learning with technology happen, and to keep my research connected to practice. Since Fall 2025, I have supported CSforGood project-based learning at Childs, Lakeview, and Unionville Elementary Schools, where interviews with teachers showed me that crafting guiding questions is the most challenging part of project-based learning (work in progress). I also volunteer each year at STEAM Night for the Monroe County Community School Corporation with pre-service teachers, and I bring semiconductor concepts to families through SILICON. Within IST, I grew from Vice President of Communication (2024–2025) to Conference Chair (2025–2026) and now President (2026–2027) of Graduate Students in Instructional Systems Technology. As chair of the 2026 IST Conference, I led its full planning, from theme and program to speakers and promotion, for nearly 100 participants. I have also served as a graduate assistant in the Teaching Technology Lab and managed the program’s Instagram account to connect current students, both on campus and online, to alumni. In the academic field, I have reviewed proposals for AECT, SITE, and the IST Conference and volunteered at the AECT International Convention. Since 2026, I have served as graduate student representative for SITE’s Instructional Design SIG. Looking ahead, I plan to review not only proposals but also journal articles in the field, join SIGs in AECT and AERA, and apply for the AECT Intern Program in 2027 to learn how a professional association serves its members and to bring my conference leadership experience to the broader field.
